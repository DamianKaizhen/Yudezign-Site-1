Core construction.

Frameless · the box · the three boards

YUDEZIGN2 of 6

No face frame. That is the whole difference.

An American cabinet has a wooden frame screwed to the front, and the door hangs off that frame. Ours does not. The hinge mounts straight to the side panel.

That frame is an inch and a half of wood on each side, sitting in front of the opening. Take it away and the opening is as wide as the box — about fifteen per cent more usable space, and drawers that are genuinely full width.

Two sentences, no diagram “Ours is European frameless — no wooden frame across the front, so the hinge goes straight onto the side panel and the opening is the full width of the box. You get about fifteen per cent more room, and the doors sit flush in one continuous line.”

The two that get missed

Why 34.5″? A 1½″ countertop lands the finished height at 36″. Say the finished number to a customer and the box number to a trade.

Bath base is 21″, not 24″. This is the single most common dimension error.

Worth practising Lay out a 12-foot L-shaped kitchen using only stock sizes in 3″ increments. Do it a few times on paper and you stop needing the table — and a rep who can sketch one on a napkin in front of a customer is worth three who go back to the office.

The shaker disclosure.

A traditional shaker door is five pieces of solid wood — two rails, two stiles, and a panel that floats so it can move with the season.

Ours is one piece of MDF with the profile machined into it, finished in RTF.

Volunteer it, in this order “Our shaker is machined MDF rather than five pieces of solid wood — and that’s on purpose. MDF doesn’t move with humidity, so the door can’t warp or split at a joint the way a wood shaker will, and it takes paint better because there’s no grain. In Houston that matters more than it would somewhere dry.”
Never Never say or imply solid wood about a shaker door. Not “solid feel,” not “solid construction.”

Overlay — the thing designers ask about.

Overlay is how much of the box the door covers. It is set by the hinge and its base plate — so a designer specifying an overlay is really specifying hardware.

InsetDoor sits inside the opening. We don’t make this
Full overlayDoor covers the panel completely. Kitchens & vanities
Half overlayTwo doors share one panel. Closets. The diagram says partial — same thing

Frameless plus full overlay is what produces the wide openings and the unbroken line across a run. Those are the two things to say when somebody asks why frameless.

What a customer is actually choosing

A swatch on a desk tells them almost nothing. Gloss reflects the room; matte absorbs it. A dark gloss doubles the apparent size of a small kitchen and shows every fingerprint. A soft-touch matte does the reverse.

Ask this before you show anything “How much natural light does that room get?”

Both of these are our own delivered work. Show ours, not stock. If a photo is not a job we built, it does not go in front of a customer.

Four ways a door opens.

OpeningWhat it is
HandlesBar pulls, arch pulls, knobs — three hardware finishes
J-pullA shaped lip on the edge of the door itself. Handleless look, no separate profile
GolaA continuous channel profile set into the carcass. The door stays plain
Push-to-openNo handle, no channel. Press and it releases
A designer will catch you on this one J-pull is not gola. Gola is a channel in the box; J-pull is a shape on the door. They look alike in a photo and they are different products at different prices. We have already mis-captioned one of our own project photos over exactly this.
Hardware finishesBrushed nickel · polished chrome · matte black
